# Literature on the coercive and controlling side of missionary medicine

A reading list to support the "trust deficit" half of the argument in *The Long Shadow of Mission Medicine* (ref. 355277/Z/26/Z). The works are grouped by the mechanism of control they describe, since each mechanism suggests a different reason why mission medicine might have left distrust behind. A counterweight section lists work stressing African agency and the popularity of mission medicine, so the argument stays balanced.

**Why this matters for the proposal.** The application supports the negative-legacy claim with Vaughan (1991) and Crozier (2007). Crozier's book is about the state's Colonial Medical Service, not missions, so a reviewer could say the mission-specific evidence for coercion is thin. Adding three or four of the starred (★) works below would close that gap.

Section 9 lists the citations whose volume or page details I could not confirm online. Check those before they go into anything you submit.

---

## 1. Framing works: colonial medicine as power

- ★ **Vaughan, M. (1991).** *Curing Their Ills: Colonial Power and African Illness.* Stanford University Press. See ch. 3, "The great dispensary in the sky: mission medicine", and ch. 4, "Without the camp: institutions and identities in the colonial history of leprosy".
  Covers British East and Central Africa. Treats biomedicine as a cultural system that constructed "the African". Chapter 3 is the core reference on mission medicine as a project to heal bodies and reform souls together.
- **Comaroff, J. (1993).** "The diseased heart of Africa: medicine, colonialism, and the black body." In S. Lindenbaum and M. Lock (eds), *Knowledge, Power, and Practice: The Anthropology of Medicine and Everyday Life*, University of California Press, pp. 305–329.
  Argues that medicine in southern Africa linked disease to supposed African moral and social disorder, which justified intervention in how people lived.
- **Comaroff, J. and Comaroff, J. (1991, 1997).** *Of Revelation and Revolution*, vols 1 and 2. University of Chicago Press.
  The "colonisation of consciousness" argument: missions reshaped bodies, homes and habits as well as beliefs. Use it as background, not as direct evidence on medicine.
- **Feierman, S. (1985).** "Struggles for control: the social roots of health and healing in modern Africa." *African Studies Review* 28(2/3): 73–147.
  A long review essay framing health and healing as contests for control between states, missions, healers and kin. You already cite it in *Healing the Nation*.
- **Marks, S. (1997).** "What is colonial about colonial medicine? And what has happened to imperialism and health?" *Social History of Medicine* 10(2): 205–219.
  Useful for a balanced position: asks how far colonial medicine was an instrument of rule and how far it was shaped by local actors.
- **Hardiman, D. (ed.) (2006).** *Healing Bodies, Saving Souls: Medical Missions in Asia and Africa.* Rodopi (Clio Medica 80).
  An edited volume on how denominations differed in their attitudes to medical work, their entanglement in local politics, and their views on supernatural cures. Includes a Kalusa chapter on Mwinilunga (see Section 8).

## 2. Care conditional on evangelism: the hospital as a site of conversion

- ★ **Ranger, T.O. (1981).** "Godly medicine: the ambiguities of medical mission in southeast Tanzania, 1900–1945." *Social Science & Medicine, Part B: Medical Anthropology* 15(3): 261–277.
  A classic on the UMCA. The mission opposed the coercion of labourers seen in industrial medicine, yet shared a paternalistic civilising agenda with other missions and tied healing to evangelisation.
- **Landau, P.S. (1996).** "Explaining surgical evangelism in colonial southern Africa: teeth, pain and faith." *Journal of African History* 37(2): 261–281.
  Surgery was used to evangelise. Missionaries presented healing as the removal of a troublesome part of the body, and this introduced a new way of talking about pain.
- **Good, C.M., Jr (2004).** *The Steamer Parish: The Rise and Fall of Missionary Medicine on an African Frontier.* University of Chicago Press.
  The UMCA's medical work around Lake Malawi. Shows how evangelical priorities shaped, and limited, what medical care the mission provided.
- **Kaduma, L. (2024).** "Medical mission futures: German Protestant missionaries and modern healthcare in Southern Highlands, Tanzania, 1891–1940." *Zamani* 1(1): 142–166. DOI: 10.56279/ZJAHS1117.
  Missionaries saw African health as a route to conversion. Most Africans used mission medicine for medical reasons and kept their own healing practices. The study spans German rule and early British Tanganyika.

## 3. Attacks on African healing and church discipline

- ★ **Hokkanen, M. (2007).** "Quests for health and contests for meaning: African church leaders and Scottish missionaries in the early twentieth-century Presbyterian Church in northern Malawi." *Journal of Southern African Studies* 33(4): 733–750. DOI: 10.1080/03057070701646811.
  How the Livingstonia church dealt with members' use of African healing, and how African church leaders contested missionary views. The same Livingstonia mission ran stations in Northern Rhodesia: Chasefu, Chitambo, Lubwa, Mwenzo and Loudon all appear in the Table 14 page you photographed, so Hokkanen's findings bear directly on stations in your dataset.
- **Hokkanen, M. (2007).** *Medicine and Scottish Missionaries in the Northern Malawi Region, 1875–1930: Quests for Health in a Colonial Society.* Edwin Mellen Press.
- **Hokkanen, M. (2017).** *Medicine, Mobility and the Empire: Nyasaland Networks, 1859–1960.* Manchester University Press. See ch. 4, "Knowledge, secrecy and contestation: early medical encounters, c.1859–c.1930".
- **Kalusa, W.T. (2017).** "African auxiliaries, cultural translation and vernacularising missionary medicine in colonial north-western Zambia." *CODESRIA Bulletin* nos 1 and 2, from p. 21.
  Medical missionaries trained Africans in scientific medicine hoping they would help destroy local medical beliefs. The auxiliaries instead translated mission medicine into local terms. Zambian, and directly about the stated aim of displacing local healing.
- **Adetiba, A. and Msindo, E. (2022).** "Chiefs and rural health services in south-western Nigeria, c. 1920–c. 1950s." *Social History of Medicine* 35(2): 589–611.
  Colonial authorities expected chiefs to enforce medical policy, while their subjects expected chiefs to defend Yoruba medical and religious practices that officials and missionaries usually undermined.
- **Flint, K.E. (2008).** *Healing Traditions: African Medicine, Cultural Exchange, and Competition in South Africa, 1820–1948.* Ohio University Press.
  Competition between African healers and biomedicine in Natal, including legal restrictions on healers.

## 4. Segregation and confinement: leprosy settlements and disease campaigns

Mission-run leprosy settlements are the clearest case of missions exercising custodial control. State sleeping-sickness campaigns, which missions sometimes supported, are the closest parallel to the Lowes and Montero setting.

- ★ **Vongsathorn, K. (2012).** "'First and foremost the evangelist'? Mission and government priorities for the treatment of leprosy in Uganda, 1927–48." *Journal of Eastern African Studies* 6(3): 544–560. Open access via PubMed Central.
  Missionaries and officials shared priorities that produced isolated, in-patient leprosy settlements, limited in scope. The system reflected a vision of a "civilised", Christian Uganda more than public-health goals. Also shows that missions and government cooperated closely here, contrary to older accounts of mutual suspicion.
- **Shankar, S. (2007).** "Medical missionaries and modernizing emirs in colonial Hausaland: leprosy control and native authority in the 1930s." *Journal of African History* 48(1): 45–68.
  Emirs strengthened their own authority by cooperating with Christian missions in the anti-leprosy campaign. Shows how mission medicine could become part of local coercive power.
- **Manton, J. (2003).** "Global and local contexts: the Northern Ogoja Leprosy Scheme, Nigeria, 1945–1960." *História, Ciências, Saúde-Manguinhos* 10(suppl. 1): 209–223.
  Leprosy control coordinated by a Catholic mission in eastern Nigeria.
- **Hoppe, K.A. (2003).** *Lords of the Fly: Sleeping Sickness Control in British East Africa, 1900–1960.* Praeger.
  Forced resettlement and population removal in Uganda and Tanganyika. A state campaign, but in British territories where missions also worked.
- **Lyons, M. (1992).** *The Colonial Disease: A Social History of Sleeping Sickness in Northern Zaire, 1900–1940.* Cambridge University Press.
  The standard account of coercive campaign medicine in central Africa: compulsory examination, isolation camps, controls on movement. Belgian and state-run, so cite it as a comparison case.

## 5. Control of women's bodies, reproduction and maternity

These works matter for your gender analysis (sex-disaggregated models, WP4 focus groups). They suggest that maternal and child health, the services you study, is where mission control was most intimate.

- ★ **Summers, C. (1991).** "Intimate colonialism: the imperial production of reproduction in Uganda, 1907–1925." *Signs* 16(4): 787–807.
  In Buganda, CMS medical missionaries and the colonial state treated venereal disease, maternity and population decline as imperial and moral problems, which brought women's reproduction under medical and moral supervision.
- **Allman, J. (1994).** "Making mothers: missionaries, medical officers and women's work in colonial Asante, 1924–1945." *History Workshop Journal* 38: 23–47.
  Missionaries and medical officers in the Gold Coast tried to remake how Asante women raised children and organised their work.
- **Hunt, N.R. (1999).** *A Colonial Lexicon of Birth Ritual, Medicalization, and Mobility in the Congo.* Duke University Press.
  A British Baptist mission hospital at Yakusu, in the Belgian Congo. Covers the medicalisation of birth, pressure to give birth in hospital, and how Congolese people reworked these interventions.
- **Thomas, L.M. (2003).** *Politics of the Womb: Women, Reproduction, and the State in Kenya.* University of California Press.
  Reproduction as a site of struggle between the state, missions and communities in Meru, including campaigns against female circumcision.
- **Murray, J. (1976).** "The Church Missionary Society and the 'female circumcision' issue in Kenya, 1929–1932." *Journal of Religion in Africa* 8(2): 92–104. DOI: 10.1163/157006676X00084.
- **Anderson, D.M. (2018).** "Women missionaries and colonial silences in Kenya's female 'circumcision' controversy, 1906–1930." *English Historical Review* 133(565), from p. 1512.
  In the 1929 to 1932 controversy, several Protestant missions made church membership and schooling conditional on renouncing female circumcision, which drove Kikuyu Christians to found independent churches and schools. This is one of the best-documented cases of mission medicine provoking organised withdrawal.
- **Dlamini, S.R. (2024).** "Maternal and child welfare at the Raleigh Fitkin Memorial Hospital and the Swazi women's response, 1933–1945." *Historia* 69(1). DOI: 10.17159/2309-8392/2024/v69n1a5.
  A Church of the Nazarene hospital in the British protectorate of Swaziland. Swazi women contested, and sometimes rejected, mission obstetric and paediatric services, forcing the missionaries to negotiate. A recent study, directly on the maternal care you measure.
- **Ratschiller Nasim, L. (2023).** *Medical Missionaries and Colonial Knowledge in West Africa and Europe, 1885–1914: Purity, Health and Cleanliness.* Palgrave Macmillan. DOI: 10.1007/978-3-031-27128-1.
  Basel Mission doctors in the Gold Coast and Cameroon. They framed syphilis in terms of sin and impurity, and some argued for racially segregated housing. Local people folded mission hygiene into their existing healing systems. Appears to be open access.

## 6. Memory, rumour and distrust

This is the closest the historical literature comes to your "trust deficit". It is especially strong for Northern Rhodesia.

- ★ **White, L. (1993).** "Vampire priests of central Africa: African debates about labor and religion in colonial northern Zambia." *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 35(4): 746–772.
  Rumours in northern Zambia that Catholic missionaries were "vampire priests". Direct evidence that some communities experienced missions as extractive and dangerous.
- ★ **White, L. (1995).** "Tsetse visions: narratives of blood and bugs in colonial Northern Rhodesia, 1931–9." *Journal of African History* 36(2): 219–245. DOI: 10.1017/S0021853700034125.
  Vampire accusations in the Northern Province in the 1930s, linked to tsetse and game control and to colonial attacks on *citemene* cultivation. Shows how colonial health interventions were read through fears of blood-taking.
- **White, L. (2000).** *Speaking with Vampires: Rumor and History in Colonial Africa.* University of California Press. See ch. 7, "Blood, bugs, and archives: debates over sleeping-sickness control in colonial Northern Rhodesia, 1931–1939".
  Across East and Central Africa, blood accusations had strongly medical meanings. In Northern Rhodesia, people said African blood was taken to cure European diseases. White also shows how rumour can be used as historical evidence, which is useful for designing WP4 interviews.

## 7. Quantitative evidence on negative legacies

- **Lowes, S. and Montero, E. (2021).** *American Economic Review* 111(4). Already cited in the application.
- ★ **Okoye, D. (2021).** "Things fall apart? Missions, institutions, and interpersonal trust." *Journal of Development Economics* 148: 102568.
  Finds a negative long-run relationship between historical missionary activity and interpersonal trust in former indirect-rule colonies, meaning ex-British colonies and Liberia. The proposed mechanism is missions' disruption of traditional institutions. This is the closest economics result to your trust argument, and it sits in your exact empirical scope, so the panel may know it. Your answer: Okoye studies interpersonal trust and missions in general, while you study institutional trust in the health system and separate medical from non-medical stations.
- **Cagé, J. and Rueda, V. (2020).** "Sex and the mission." *Journal of Demographic Economics* 86(3). Already cited. Living near mission settlements raises HIV today, while living near mission health facilities lowers it: one mission legacy, two directions.
- **Chiseni, M.C. (2024).** "The sins of the church." *Economic History of Developing Regions* 39(1). Your own evidence that missionary practice left adverse health-behaviour legacies.

## 8. Counterweight: African agency and the popularity of mission medicine

Cite these alongside the works above, so the panel sees that the hypothesis allows both a dividend and a deficit.

- **Kalusa, W.T. (2006).** "Christian medical discourse and praxis on the imperial frontier: explaining the popularity of missionary medicine in Mwinilunga District, Zambia, 1906–1935." In Hardiman (ed.), *Healing Bodies, Saving Souls*.
- **Kalusa, W.T. (2014).** "Missionaries, African patients, and negotiating missionary medicine at Kalene Hospital, Zambia, 1906–1935." *Journal of Southern African Studies* 40(2).
  Kalene Hill, run by Christian Missions in Many Lands, appears in the Table 14 page you photographed. Kalusa's work therefore maps directly onto a station in your dataset.
- **Kalusa (2007), Jennings (2008), Doyle, Meier zu Selhausen and Weisdorf (2020).** Already cited in the application.

## 9. How to use this in the proposal and interview

1. **Fix the citation gap.** Where the application says mission medicine was "entangled with coercion, social control, and the disruption of existing healing systems", cite Ranger (1981), Hokkanen (2007, JSAS), Vongsathorn (2012) and White (1993) next to Vaughan (1991). Keep Crozier (2007) for the state side.
2. **Turn "coercion" into something you can measure.** The literature points to specific practices that could be coded as a separate "coercive exposure" next to "care exposure": mission-run leprosy settlements, mission involvement in state campaigns (sleeping sickness, venereal disease, vaccination), maternity and infant-welfare programmes, and denominational rules on healers and initiation. Check whether the medical reports and church returns record these. If they do, this gives a concrete answer to interview Q5 (when should a deficit appear rather than a dividend?).
3. **Design WP4 questions around stories, not ratings.** White's work shows that distrust of colonial medicine was often voiced as rumour. Asking "what did people say about the hospital?" may surface negative memories that "did you trust the hospital?" would not.
4. **One-line answer for the panel:** "The historical literature documents real coercion in mission medicine, especially in leprosy settlements, maternity programmes and attacks on healers, and also real popularity. My design separates these exposures so the data can show which left the stronger mark."
