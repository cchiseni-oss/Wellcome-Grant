Research proposal summary
Research summary
In Sub-Saharan Africa, formal health facilities exist and are physically reachable,
yet many communities use them little, especially for preventive and maternal care.
Standard explanations (poverty, distance, facility quality) leave much of this gap
unexplained. This project tests a historical explanation: that the colonial geography
of Christian missionary medicine, the dominant provider of formal healthcare in
British colonial Africa from the late nineteenth century, shaped enduring
differences in institutional trust, the willingness of communities to engage with
formal health systems, and that this trust gradient still helps explain why disease
burden is distributed so unevenly across space today.
I will build the first comprehensive, geo-referenced dataset of colonial missionary
stations and their medical facilities across British colonial Africa, linking mission-
station locations from the ecclesiastical returns in the Colonial Blue Books to
station-level, annual medical activity (in- and out-patient volumes) recorded in the
Colonial Medical Reports, extending the method I developed and validated in my
PhD for Zambia (1924 to 1953). Linking it to contemporary survey, facility, and
spatial disease data, and using a formal mediation design supported by
community fieldwork in Zambia, I will test whether, and through what channels,
colonial health history still shapes the spatial burden of disease.

Your research vision
Research vision
The problem
In much of Sub-Saharan Africa there is a puzzle that remains unresolved in the health-
systems literature. Formal facilities are often within physical reach, yet utilisation of
preventive, antenatal, and child health services remains low, and disease burden is high
and spatially uneven (Weiss et al. 2019; Local Burden of Disease Collaborators 2020). The
standard explanations are supply-side and contemporary: poverty, distance, facility
density and quality, availability of staff and drugs (Peters et al. 2008; Ensor and Cooper
2004). These matter, but a residual remains. Communities similar on income, education,
and distance to the nearest clinic still differ markedly in how readily they engage with
formal health systems (Ensor and Cooper 2004). My central claim is that part of this
residual is historical, running through institutional trust, the degree to which a community
treats the formal health system as a legitimate, reliable institution worth engaging with.
Trust is now recognised as a foundational determinant of health-system performance.
Gilson argues that trust is constitutive of healthcare as a social institution, shaping
whether people present early, disclose honestly, accept advice, and return (Gilson 2003,
2005). Where institutional trust is low, people delay care, bypass nearer facilities, default
on treatment, and rely on informal providers, with measurable consequences for
infectious and chronic disease control (Østergaard 2015; Arakelyan et al. 2021). But the
literature treats trust as essentially contemporary, and lacks a historical account of why it
varies significantly across space. That is the gap this project fills.
The historical argument
In British colonial Africa, the formal healthcare a community first encountered was mainly
missionary (Chiseni 2025). From the late nineteenth century, Christian missionary
societies were the dominant providers of hospitals, dispensaries, and trained medical
staff, decades before colonial states built meaningful public health systems (Vaughan
1991; Hokkanen 2019; Doyle et al. 2020). By the early twentieth century Protestant
medical missions were treating millions of patients a year (Calvi and Mantovanelli 2018).
The encounter was uneven. In some places missionary medicine was experienced as
care, building familiarity and a durable disposition to seek formal treatment (Jennings
2008); in others it was entangled with coercion, social control, and the disruption of
existing healing systems, leaving suspicion that could outlast the missions themselves
(Vaughan 1991; Crozier 2007).
My argument is that this colonial geography of missionary medicine laid down an enduring
substrate of institutional trust, positive in some places, negative in others, still shaping

health-seeking behaviour and, through it, today's spatial distribution of disease burden.
The economic-history literature shows colonial-era institutions leave persistent imprints
on education (Wantchekon et al. 2015; Nunn 2014), human capital and culture (Valencia
Caicedo 2019), trust after the slave trade (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011), and, closest to my
work, attitudes to medicine: Lowes and Montero (2021) show that violent colonial medical
campaigns in central Africa left a persistent legacy of distrust and lower vaccination
uptake. Conversely, Calvi and Mantovanelli (2018) show that proximity to a Protestant
medical mission in colonial India predicts better health outcomes today, through durable
changes in habits and engagement rather than surviving infrastructure or conversion.
Colonial medicine could leave either a trust dividend or a trust deficit. No study has
measured this legacy at scale across British colonial Africa, identified missionary
medicine as its source, or tested institutional trust as the channel through which it still
shapes disease burden.
Aims and research questions
The programme is organised around four linked questions.
RQ1 (historical map). Where was colonial missionary health infrastructure located across
British colonial Africa, and can it be reconstructed at the facility level, individual hospitals
and dispensaries, rather than the coarse mission-station points the existing literature
relies on, with variation by denomination, territory, and period?
RQ2 (trust, behaviour, and burden). Do communities historically exposed to missionary
health infrastructure show systematically different institutional trust and health-seeking
behaviour today than comparable communities that were not, and does that difference,
through behaviour, predict a measurable, spatially patterned disease-burden penalty?
RQ3 (mechanisms). Through what channels is this inheritance transmitted? Candidate
mechanisms include the survival of faith-based facilities as mediators of the community-
state health relationship; intergenerational transmission of health-worker norms; the
embedding of health-seeking as a social and religious norm; and, conversely, the legacy
of coercive colonial medical campaigns experienced as social control.
RQ4 (community meaning). How do people in communities with different colonial health
histories themselves understand their relationship with formal healthcare? This question
deliberately centres the values, memory, and priorities of communities rarely asked to
explain their own health-seeking behaviour in historical terms.
Why this is bold

The project reframes a problem the health-systems field treats as contemporary and
supply-side as one that is also historical and demand-side. The economic-history
literature has shown colonial-era institutions leave persistent imprints, but has measured
outcomes (schooling, income, height, HIV, vaccination) rather than the behavioural
pathway of community engagement with health systems, and has not tested missionary
medicine at scale across British colonial Africa. No study has joined these fields: linking
the colonial geography of missionary medicine to present-day institutional trust and
health-seeking behaviour, and then asking whether that trust gradient is itself the channel
through which colonial history still shapes the spatial distribution of disease burden. If the
answer is yes, it changes how we explain a major component of health inequality on the
continent, and identifies a lever, trust, that infrastructure spending alone will not move.
Why this is creative
The creativity lies in the combination and in the source material. I bring together four
bodies of evidence never previously integrated: (i) an original, facility-level historical map
of mission medicine; (ii) georeferenced contemporary survey data on behaviour and trust;
(iii) high-resolution spatial disease-burden estimates; and (iv) community testimony from
qualitative fieldwork. The analytical move tying them together is a formal mediation
design: rather than merely correlating historical exposure with present outcomes, I
decompose the effect of mission exposure on disease burden into shares running through
institutional trust, through health-seeking behaviour, and any residual direct effect (for
example, surviving infrastructure). This turns a plausible historical story into a testable
causal pathway. The qualitative component is the instrument for identifying which
mechanisms operate and for letting communities articulate meanings no survey captures,
including negative legacies a naive "missions were good for health" reading would miss.
A second source of novelty is the historical data itself. Almost all quantitative work on
African missions relies on digitised Christian missionary atlases, Beach (1903), Roome
(1925), Streit, as in Cagé and Rueda (2016) and Brown's work on Bible translation, which
digitises four Protestant and two Catholic atlases (Brown 2025). These atlases are
invaluable but demonstrably incomplete. Jedwab et al. (2022) show, by comparing the
commonly used atlases against ecclesiastical returns and other primary sources, that
atlases omit 76 to 91% of missions across Sub-Saharan Africa and up to 98% in Ghana,
systematically over-representing prominent, early, European-staffed "residence" missions
while missing the hinterland, thereby biasing estimates of mission effects. My dataset will
be built from the same family of comprehensive administrative sources that Jedwab et al.
identify as the corrective to atlases, and extends it to health provision through two
complementary streams. The first, the ecclesiastical returns in the colonial Blue Books,
logs missionary station provision society by society, the kind Jedwab et al. show to be far
more complete than any atlas. The second, the Colonial Medical Reports, is a source not
previously exploited for this purpose: it records subsidised mission (and government)
hospitals and dispensaries with station-level annual patient volumes, adding a health-
facility layer that no atlas or existing mission dataset captures. I have done this for Zambia

in my PhD, geo-referencing station locations from the ecclesiastical returns (1924 to 1948)
and mission hospitals from the medical reports (1933 to 1953). Scaling this method across
British colonial Africa will produce a dataset more comprehensive and accurate than any
atlas-based source, a contribution independent of my analysis.
Design and work packages (48 months)
The empirical scope is British colonial Africa, where the administrative sources (the Blue
Books and the Colonial Medical Reports) form a single, comparable series; contemporary
outcomes are analysed across the former British colonies, where survey coverage is also
strongest.
WP1: The dataset (months 1 to 18). I will build a geo-referenced dataset of colonial
missionary stations and their medical facilities across British colonial Africa from two
complementary sources. The ecclesiastical returns in the Blue Books record mission-
station locations by society, giving the full station universe including non-medical stations.
The Colonial Medical Reports record subsidised mission hospitals and dispensaries, their
operating society, and station-level annual patient volumes. Joining the two lets me flag
which stations operated a medical facility, distinguish a hospital from a dispensary, and
measure intensity and duration of medical activity rather than mere presence. Moreover,
each record carries operating society, facility type, years of activity, patient volumes
where available, and provenance. The dataset will be validated against Cagé and Rueda's
(2016, 2020) atlas data, the "Mapping Missions" data (Hedde-von Westernhagen and
Becker 2022), and Brown's (2025) digitised atlases, explicitly quantifying the atlas-
omission problem documented by Jedwab et al. (2022), and released open access.
Sources are held at the British Library, LSE (Colonial Records), the National Archives
(Kew), SOAS (LMS, CMS, Methodist Missionary Society, and White Fathers records), and
the Bodleian (Rhodes House collections), supplemented by the British Online Archives. My
Zambian dataset provides the coding protocol and benchmark.
WP2: Trust, behaviour, and burden (months 12 to 30). IPUMS-Demographic Health
Survey (DHS) provides individual-level, cluster-georeferenced measures of facility
utilisation, antenatal-care attendance, vaccination uptake, care-seeking for childhood
illness, and place of delivery across many rounds, linkable to historical exposure at the
(displaced) cluster level (Minnesota Population Center 2022). Afrobarometer Round 9
(2021 to 2023; 53,444 respondents across 39 countries), with earlier rounds, captures
experiential and trust dimensions directly: contact with public clinics, ease of obtaining
care, experiences of disrespect and informal payment, and institutional trust items
relevant to health. Disease burden is measured from high-resolution Institute for Health
Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) Local Burden of Disease estimates at roughly 5 by 5 km for
malaria, child illness, and related outcomes (DALYs at sub-national administrative level),
aggregated to the same cluster geography as the behavioural outcomes. Contemporary
facility access is controlled using the KEMRI-Wellcome and WHO database of 98,745
public health facilities across 50 Sub-Saharan African countries (Maina et al. 2019),

whose faith-based-ownership flag also identifies likely descendants of former mission
hospitals, a key mediating variable, supplemented by the WHO AFRO Geodatabase. Care
will be taken to model what each source actually measures: Afrobarometer captures
access and experience rather than an explicit "trust in health facilities" item, and IHME
provides prevalence and mortality at 5 by 5 km but DALYs at administrative level, and
measures will be constructed accordingly.
WP3: Identification and mechanisms (months 24 to 42). The design extends credible-
identification strategies established for this literature, combining matching on observable
determinants of mission location with an instrumental-variable strategy that exploits
differences across missionary societies in their propensity to undertake medical (rather
than evangelical or educational) work, following Calvi and Mantovanelli (2018) and Cagé
and Rueda (2016). Joining the station universe to the medical facilities distinguishes
medical missions from missions without health facilities, the latter serving as a placebo to
isolate the health-specific channel. Because Jedwab et al. (2022) show mission
placement favoured healthier, more accessible, and richer locations, I control directly for
the determinants they identify (historical malaria, railways, cash-crop suitability, urban
access), country fixed effects, and for historical state provision using government-
hospital data from Bolt and Cilliers (2025), so the missionary effect is separated from
siting and from where the colonial state invested. Identification is probed with placebo,
distance-based falsification, Oster-bound, and alternative-exposure tests. Mechanism
analysis then proceeds in three stages: first, testing faith-based facility ownership as a
mediating pathway to contemporary trust, given that faith-based organisations remain
major providers across Sub-Saharan Africa (Olivier et al. 2015); second, qualitative
process tracing in the Zambian case studies (WP4) to identify the channels (health-
worker lineages, religious norms, collective memory) through which experience is
transmitted, building on evidence that African auxiliaries reinterpreted and carried
missionary medicine forward locally (Kalusa 2007); and third, once WP2 linkage and WP4
coding are complete (month 32), formal mediation analysis decomposing the exposure
effect on disease burden into shares operating through trust, behaviour, and a residual
direct effect.
WP4: Community meaning (months 20 to 32). Qualitative fieldwork will be carried out in
four purposively selected Zambian communities: two historically served by missionary
hospitals through the colonial period, and two without such exposure but with comparable
contemporary facility access. Semi-structured interviews with community health workers,
local officials, traditional leaders, elderly residents with living memory of mission
medicine, and younger residents will explore how colonial health histories are
remembered, transmitted, and expressed today. Sex-disaggregated focus groups will
provide direct evidence on how gender shapes the inheritance of colonial health
relationships, given the strongly gendered character of missionary maternal medicine.
Communities will help interpret and validate findings and shape policy outputs. Building on
my Gothenburg University postdoc fieldwork, this work surfaces community-articulated

meanings, including negative legacies, that quantitative data alone cannot capture.
WP5: Synthesis and dissemination (months 42 to 48). Mixed-methods integration of
quantitative and qualitative findings, completion and revision of the disease-burden and

Feasibility and risk
The programme is ambitious but feasible because its hardest component, archival
construction of a geo-referenced missionary health dataset from ecclesiastical and
administrative records, is the work I have already done for Zambia. The methods (archival
reconstruction, geocoding, matching and IV identification, mixed-methods fieldwork) are
established in my track record. A research assistant will support digitisation and
geocoding, de-risking the timeline and strengthening capacity building. Headline results
are produced on best-covered territories first, so central findings do not depend on
complete coverage. Bolt and Cilliers (2025) show multi-territory digitisation is tractable,
having built comparable state-healthcare data for twelve British African colonies from the
same records.
The principal risks are addressed by design. Archival coverage varies by territory: I
mitigate this by phasing coverage (richest sources first), recording provenance, validating
against existing point datasets, and structuring the analysis so core results hold on best-
covered territories before extension. Mission-placement endogeneity is addressed by
adopting the controls and identification strategies of Jedwab et al. (2022), Calvi and
Mantovanelli (2018), and Cagé and Rueda (2016). Measurement mismatch is handled by
modelling each variable for what it actually captures. DHS cluster coordinates are
deliberately displaced; I will use established methods for exposure measurement under
positional uncertainty (buffer zones, distance bands, sensitivity to displacement radius).
Because some outcomes are spatial aggregates, I will retain individual-level estimation
where possible and avoid over-claiming at the individual level from grid-cell burden data.
The aim is a credible, transparent, well-bounded estimate, not a false claim of perfect
exogeneity.
## Background
This project directly extends my published research. My doctoral work at Lund University
(Here I Am, Send Me, 2022; open-access monograph, Springer 2024) established that the
legacy of Christian missions in Zambia can be measured precisely and linked to long-run
outcomes. From colonial government reports, missionary society administrative records,
and published medical-mission surveys, I built an original geo-referenced dataset of
missionary health stations in Zambia. That dataset underpins two peer-reviewed
contributions proving the two halves of this proposal. In African Studies (Chiseni 2025), I
show how missionary colonial healthcare and disease-prevention activity can be
reconstructed at fine spatial and temporal resolution from archival sources, the method I
10Research proposal

will scale up. In Economic History of Developing Regions (Chiseni 2024), I demonstrate a
credible identification strategy linking colonial missionary exposure to a contemporary
health outcome (HIV and sexual behaviour), showing the long-run causal-inference
approach works for health behaviour, not just education or income. I have also led six
months of primary qualitative fieldwork in rural Zambia, so the community-facing
component rests on demonstrated capacity.
The wider evidence base motivates the design. Spatial inequalities in health within African
countries are often as large as those between them (Weiss et al. 2019; Local Burden of
Disease Collaborators 2020). Trust is repeatedly identified as a mediator of demand that
supply-side models miss (Gilson 2003, 2005; Østergaard 2015). Lowes and Montero
(2021) show coercive colonial medicine can depress trust and uptake decades later. My
project asks the complementary, larger question for the missionary health system across
British colonial Africa, pushing through trust to disease burden.

Expected outcomes
The project will deliver the following: (1) the first openly available, facility-level dataset of
colonial missionary health infrastructure across British colonial Africa, more complete
than atlas-based sources and an enabling resource for a generation of researchers; (2)
the first large-scale evidence on whether colonial missionary medicine shaped
contemporary institutional trust and, through it, disease burden; (3) a demand-side,

qualitative evidence on how health histories are lived and remembered; and (5) policy-
relevant findings on where low utilisation reflects a historically rooted trust deficit rather
than poverty or distance, with implications for how health communication, community-
health-worker programmes, and facility governance should be designed. The programme
also establishes me as an independent leader bridging economic history, health-systems
research, and spatial epidemiology.

Details of study design for research involving human participants
This is a mixed-methods observational study with no intervention, manipulation, or clinical
procedure. It has two empirical components.
(1) Secondary quantitative analysis. This component links the project's new historical
dataset (WP1) to existing, de-identified, georeferenced survey microdata; it involves no
recruitment, intervention, or new data collection. From the DHS, the relevant samples are
women of reproductive age and their children across Sub-Saharan African rounds,
providing health-seeking outcomes (facility delivery, antenatal care, vaccination, care-
seeking). From Afrobarometer, the relevant respondents are nationally representative
adult samples, providing measures of healthcare contact, experience, and institutional
trust. Disease-burden outcomes are modelled IHME estimates at the cluster level.
"Exposure" is the historical missionary-health infrastructure attached to a respondent's
georeferenced cluster, not an assigned condition. Sex and gender are treated as
substantive variables, with sex-disaggregated models throughout, given the gendered
character of missionary maternal medicine. Because participants are not recruited, the
key statistical consideration is the precision of the linked-cluster estimates, which is high
given the scale of these datasets. Bias is addressed through pre-specified models,
country fixed effects, falsification and placebo tests, sensitivity bounds, and robustness to
cluster-coordinate displacement.
(2) Primary qualitative fieldwork (Zambia). Four purposively selected communities (two
historically mission-served, two comparison) will host approximately 40 to 60 semi-
structured interviews in total (community health workers, local health officials, traditional
leaders, elderly residents with living memory of mission medicine, and younger residents)
and 8 focus-group discussions (sex-disaggregated, two per community). Interviews and
focus groups last roughly 45 to 90 minutes, audio-recorded with consent, transcribed
and, where needed, translated. This is participant observation and interviewing only; there
are no biological samples, screening procedures, biometric measures, or behavioural
treatments. Follow-up is limited to a community-validation visit to share and check
interpretations. The only activity involving potential sensitivity is the discussion of
historical experiences of colonial medicine. These interviews will be conducted by
trained, locally embedded researchers using culturally appropriate protocols developed
with community leaders. Participants will have clear rights to pause or withdraw at any
point, ensuring that the process remains entirely voluntary and respectful. The country
where primary research takes place is Zambia; secondary data span Sub-Saharan Africa.

Outline your strategy for recruitment and describe the inclusion or exclusion criteria
for study participants (if applicable).
For the qualitative component, recruitment is purposive and community-led. Communities
are selected on historical missionary-health exposure and comparable contemporary
facility access (using the KEMRI-WHO facility database and my historical dataset). Within

leadership, and community health workers, with deliberate sampling for age (to capture
living memory and younger perspectives), sex (sex-disaggregated focus groups), and

consent; for the "living memory" stratum, older residents with direct or family recollection
of mission-era healthcare. Exclusion: individuals unable to give informed consent; and, for
safeguarding, anyone for whom participation would pose distress. To avoid elite capture
and ensure under-served voices are heard, I will sample beyond leadership networks (for
example, women's groups, peripheral households) and offer interviews in local languages.
The quantitative component uses existing survey samples and inherits their nationally
representative sampling frames; no separate recruitment occurs.

Justification for staff
The most labour-intensive phases of the project are the systematic digitisation and
geocoding of colonial missionary station and health-facility records (WP1, months 1 to 18),
and the subsequent linkage of this historical dataset to contemporary georeferenced
survey and disease-burden data (WP2, months 12 to 30). For Zambia alone, the
digitisation required coding several hundred station-year and facility-year observations
from printed archival tables, verifying location names and assigning geographic
coordinates using GIS software. Scaling the same method across the remaining British
colonial territories multiplies this task. The data-linkage phase then requires matching
historical exposure at the cluster level to DHS, Afrobarometer, and IHME datasets,
constructing spatial exposure measures under different definitions, and running
robustness checks on spatial matching.
The postgraduate research assistant will be recruited for 24 months (months 1 to 24),
covering both the intensive archival-digitisation phase (WP1) and the data-linkage phase
(WP2). During months 2 to 4, the research assistant will accompany me to London for 3
weeks to conduct parallel archival photography across multiple UK archives, accelerating
the capture of records that form the basis of all subsequent coding and geocoding work.
Their responsibilities will include but not be limited to: (i) systematic extraction and coding
of mission-station and hospital records from archival sources, following the validated
Zambian coding protocol I developed in my PhD; (ii) geocoding of facility locations using
historical maps, gazetteers, and GIS; (iii) data cleaning, cross-validation against existing
datasets (Cagé and Rueda; Hedde-von Westernhagen and Becker 2022; Brown 2025),
and provenance documentation; (iv) preparation of the dataset for open-access release,
including metadata and codebook drafting; and (v) spatial linkage of the historical dataset
to contemporary DHS, Afrobarometer, and IHME data at the cluster level, including
construction of alternative exposure measures and robustness checks on spatial
matching.

Justification for materials and consumables
Materials and consumables (ZAR 19, 600)
The dataset-construction phase (WP1) requires systematic, high-volume photography of
colonial Blue Book ecclesiastical returns and Colonial Medical Reports across five UK
archives during a concentrated archival trip. Both the PI (6 weeks) and the research
assistant (3 weeks) will work simultaneously at different archives to maximise coverage

Portable copy stands or overhead tripods, 2 units (ZAR 4200). Lightweight, foldable
copy stands that hold the camera at a fixed distance and angle above the document,
ensuring consistent framing, focus, and image quality across thousands of pages. A stand
eliminates blur and distortion from handheld photography and significantly increases
capture speed during long archive sessions.
Remote shutter releases or tethering cables, 2 units (ZAR 1700). Remote triggers allow
the photographer to capture images without touching the camera, eliminating vibration
when mounted on the copy stand and improving sharpness across high-volume sessions.
Portable LED panels, 2 units (ZAR 1700). Small, battery-powered light panels provide
supplementary even illumination in reading rooms where natural or overhead lighting is
uneven, reducing shadows across bound volumes and improving legibility of captured
images. Use will be confirmed with each archive's photography policy.

ecclesiastical records and historical administration logs. This access allows the research
team to map out, catalog, and pre-index data fields from the host institution before
embarking on physical archival travel. This preparation minimizes time spent abroad,
optimizes the on-site workflow, and provides a continuous digital verification loop during
the coding process.

RE: Indirect costs on Wellcome Trust Award Application, Michael Chanda Chiseni
Stellenbosch University (SU) researchers are committed to making their expertise available to South African
and international organisations. Research funding obtained in this way is managed by the University with the
utmost diligence.
- All university activities that are funded from external funding are associated with certain
indirect costs, and the motivation for the Indirect Cost Recovery Rate (ICRR) is to recover
these  indirect  costs  and  thereby  to  manage  the  University  as  a  financially  sustainable
enterprise.

A further aspect contributing to the need for indirect cost recovery is the South African
legislation with regards to intellectual property - the Intellectual Property Rights from Publicly
Financed Research and Development Act, No. 51 of 2008 (hereinafter ‘the IPR Act’).  The
IPR Act requires institutions that receive public funding to recover the full cost with regard
to contract research.

With reference to the abovementioned legislation, a national approach was designed and
approved with regard to the calculation of the ICRR for all public higher education institutions
in South Africa. SU uses the approved national formula and actual information from its financial
statements to calculate the ICRR in an accurate and nuanced manner. The ICRR policy and
ICRR percentage went through a second review in 2022. The amended ICRR policy and ICRR
percentage was approved, and the new policy has been effective as of 1 January 2023.
