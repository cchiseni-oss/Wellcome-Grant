## 1. What the panel is looking for

The panel is **generalist**. Expect a psychologist, a biologist, an epidemiologist and perhaps one social scientist, so explain everything in plain terms. Every member will have read your proposal and the external reviews.

Wellcome's interview guidance says you should be ready to:

- explain why your skills and experience make you the right applicant
- show that you own the project
- explain why it matters and how it could change the field
- justify your choices of methods and collaborators
- explain the risks, your back-up plans and mitigations
- place the project among the latest advances in your field
- discuss how you have shaped, or will help build, a positive and inclusive research environment

According to the scheme page, Wellcome expects Early-Career Award holders to use the award to widen their technical skills or methods, build a collaborative network, develop people-management skills, and learn to do research responsibly while promoting a positive, inclusive culture. By the end, holders should be ready to lead an independent programme. Each of these should show up at least once in your presentation or your answers.

**Answer technique for the 20-minute Q&A.** Expect 8 to 14 questions. For each:

1. Give the answer in one sentence.
2. Give one reason or one piece of evidence.
3. Stop. Aim for 30 to 60 seconds.

If a question has two parts, say "There are two parts to that" and number them. If you don't know, say so, then say how you would find out. If a question is unclear, ask them to repeat it; the guidance explicitly allows this.

## 6. Likely questions and model answers

The answers are written in the first person, sized for 30 to 60 seconds. Rephrase them in your own words; memorised answers sound memorised. ★ marks the questions most likely to come up. Drill those first.

### A. The big picture (generalist panel)

**Q1 ★ Explain your project in one minute to someone outside your field.**
Across Africa, many people live near a clinic but still don't use it for antenatal care, childbirth or vaccination. Poverty and distance explain part of that, not all. I think part of the rest is history. For decades, the first Western healthcare most communities met was run by missionaries. Where that experience was caring and lasting, people may have learned to trust formal care; where it was coercive, they may have learned the opposite. I'll map every mission hospital and dispensary in British colonial Africa from colonial records, link that map to today's surveys and disease data, and test whether trust is the link. In Zambia I'll also ask communities how they remember this history.

**Q2 ★ Why does history matter for a health problem today? Why not just fix today's clinics?**
Because if part of low use is a trust problem, building or equipping clinics won't fix that part. History helps us find where a trust deficit is likely and understand why it exists. The point isn't to change the past; it's to diagnose the present correctly. Where low use comes from distrust, the right tools differ: community health workers recruited locally, partnerships with trusted church providers, respectful-care programmes. This matters now: the new malaria vaccines need four doses, so they depend on families coming back to the clinic again and again.

**Q3 ★ What's your main expected finding, and what changes if you're right?**
My main hypothesis is that sustained mission medical provision left a positive legacy on average: more engagement with formal care today, showing up as lower burden for conditions where timely care matters, such as childhood illness. If trust carries a meaningful share of that, it changes how we explain within-country health inequality. It also tells planners where trust-building, not more infrastructure, is the right investment.

**Q4 What if you find nothing?**
A well-identified null is informative. It would mean the utilisation gap is mainly a present-day problem, not a historical one, which matters for policy and fits the evidence from Jedwab and colleagues that mission effects shrink with better data. The dataset stands regardless as an open resource for anyone working on colonial health. I'll pre-register the main specifications so that a null result gets published.

**Q5 ★ Your theory allows both a positive and a negative legacy. Isn't any result consistent with it? How could you be wrong?**
I'll pre-specify when each sign should appear. A trust dividend should be strongest where provision was long-lasting, high-volume, staffed by African auxiliaries, and where a church facility still operates. A deficit should appear where mission medicine was tied to coercive campaigns, or was short-lived. On average I expect a positive effect, as Calvi and Mantovanelli found in India. I'd be wrong if the dividend appears where provision was brief, if effects don't rise with intensity, or if missions without medical facilities show the same "health" effect as those with them.

### B. Novelty and the latest advances

**Q6 ★ How is this different from Cagé and Rueda (2020), Calvi and Mantovanelli (2018) and Lowes and Montero (2021)?**
Three differences. Data: Cagé and Rueda use the 1903 Protestant atlas, a single snapshot that Jedwab and colleagues show misses most missions. I use administrative sources covering Protestant and Catholic societies, with facility type, years of operation and patient volumes. Outcome: they study HIV, Calvi and Mantovanelli study health in India, and Lowes and Montero study trust after French *state* campaigns. I study engagement with the health system and disease burden. Mechanism: nobody has tested trust as the channel for the mission legacy, or measured how much of the effect runs through it.

**Q7 Jedwab and colleagues find mission effects shrink sharply with better data and controls. Why would yours survive?**
I've built the project around their critique. Their finding is that atlas data and thin controls inflate effects, so I use the same kind of complete sources they recommend, plus their controls. My main comparison is also less exposed to their bias: medical and non-medical stations went through the same selection into location, so comparing them isolates the medical part. If the effect shrinks to zero under those conditions, that's an important finding in itself.

**Q8 What are the latest advances you're building on?**
Four strands. Measurement: Jedwab and colleagues on atlas omissions, and Bolt and Cilliers' new data on colonial state hospitals in twelve colonies, which I use as a control. Medical mistrust: Lowes and Montero in central Africa; Alsan and Wanamaker on the Tuskegee study and Black men's health in the US; Martinez-Bravo and Stegmann on vaccination after the CIA's fake vaccination campaign in Pakistan. Together they show a single historical event can shift trust and health behaviour for decades. High-resolution burden mapping from IHME and the Malaria Atlas Project. And methods: Kelly's work on spatial noise in persistence studies, and modern sensitivity analysis for mediation.

### D. Measuring trust, behaviour and burden

**Q15 ★ Trust is your central concept, but Afrobarometer has no direct "trust in health facilities" question. How do you measure it?**
Three ways, so I don't depend on one survey item. First, revealed trust in the DHS: refusing a free blood test, which Lowes and Montero used as a measure of trust in medicine, and which sits in the same sample as the health behaviours. Second, Afrobarometer items on experiences at public clinics (how easy it was to get care, disrespect, informal payments), compared with trust in other institutions. If the mission legacy is about medicine, it should show up for health, not for trust in the police. Third, how people describe trust in the fieldwork. Following Gilson, I separate trust in biomedicine, in health workers, and in the health system.

**Q16 ★ IHME burden estimates are model outputs that already use covariates and DHS data. Isn't regressing them on history partly circular?**
It's a fair concern, so I treat IHME as secondary, mainly for mapping. My primary outcomes are measured directly in the DHS for the same households: child mortality from birth histories, vaccination, care-seeking, and malaria test and anaemia results where collected. When I use IHME estimates, I carry their uncertainty intervals through the analysis and check that results aren't driven by covariates the models themselves use, such as travel time.

**Q17 Malaria is mostly ecological. Why would trust matter for it?**
Prevalence is largely ecological, which is why I control for historical malaria suitability, as Jedwab and colleagues do. Trust matters for the parts of malaria that depend on care: prompt treatment of fever, severe illness and death, and now completing the vaccine schedule. Those are the outcomes I'll focus on.

**Q18 How do you define exposure, given that DHS coordinates are displaced?**
Several ways: distance to the nearest historical mission medical facility; presence within distance bands such as 10 and 25 km; years of operation; and patient intensity. DHS displaces urban clusters by up to 2 km and rural clusters by up to 5 km (1% up to 10 km), so I use bands wider than the displacement, re-run results with simulated displacement, and avoid fine thresholds.

**Q19 Trust may simply reflect the quality of today's facilities, many of which descend from mission hospitals. How do you separate history from current quality?**
That's one of the mechanisms I want to measure. If mission hospitals survive as good church facilities and people trust them, that's the surviving-institution channel. I separate it from a memory channel by comparing places where the mission facility survives with places where it closed, and by using facility-quality data where it exists, such as the World Bank Service Delivery Indicators surveys. The fieldwork helps too: people can tell us whether they trust a clinic for what it is now or for what it was.

### E. Separating cause from selection

**Q20 ★ Missions chose where to go. Why isn't this just selection?**
Missions did go to healthier, more accessible, richer places first. I tackle that in layers: controls for the location drivers Jedwab and colleagues identify; matching; comparing medical with non-medical stations, which faced the same choice of location; an instrument based on societies' medical propensity; and dose-response tests. I then report how strong unobserved selection would need to be to explain the result away, using Oster bounds. I'm not claiming perfect exogeneity. I'm aiming for an estimate whose limits are clear.

**Q21 ★ What exactly is your instrument, and why does it meet the exclusion restriction? Your own HIV paper suggests missionary practice affected sexual behaviour, which could affect health directly.**
The instrument is the medical propensity of the society that held a station: the share of that society's stations with a medical facility in *other* territories, leaving out the station's own territory so local conditions don't drive it. The first stage is that medically minded societies opened hospitals more often at any given station. The exclusion concern is real: my own HIV paper shows that missionary practice can shape health behaviour directly. So I control for denomination and for school provision, check that pre-mission characteristics are balanced across societies, and report plausibly-exogenous bounds (Conley, Hansen and Rossi) showing how large a direct effect of society identity would have to be to overturn the estimate. The instrument is one strand of evidence, not the whole case.

**Q22 Medical stations were probably bigger, earlier and more European-staffed than non-medical ones. Is that comparison fair?**
Not without adjustment. The church returns record station type, staff and schools, so I compare medical and non-medical stations of similar size, type and founding date, and test balance on pre-mission characteristics. Jedwab and colleagues show that main, early, European-staffed stations differ, so I control for exactly those features.

**Q23 You control for contemporary facility access. Isn't that a "bad control", since today's facilities are partly an outcome of missions?**
Yes, and I'll make the distinction explicit. The main estimate leaves contemporary facility access out. I then add facility access and faith-based ownership as mediators, which tells me how much of the effect runs through surviving infrastructure. This will be stated in the pre-analysis plan.

**Q24 Persistence studies can produce spurious results through spatial autocorrelation. How do you handle that?**
Conley standard errors with wide distance cut-offs, reporting residual spatial autocorrelation, and Kelly's spatial-noise test, which checks whether random spatial noise could produce "effects" as large as mine. Jedwab and colleagues do the same.

**Q25 So much has happened since independence: nationalisation, conflict, HIV, structural adjustment, Pentecostal growth. How can a colonial effect survive and be identified?**
Country fixed effects absorb national events such as health policy after independence or structural adjustment. Within countries, I control for subnational factors such as conflict exposure and HIV prevalence, and look at variation, for example whether a mission hospital stayed with the church or was taken over by the state. The question isn't whether history is the biggest factor; it's whether a measurable share survives.

**Q26 Missions often worked alongside the colonial state in epidemic campaigns. How do you separate mission care from state coercion?**
The medical reports separate government and mission facilities and describe government campaigns, so I can code where state campaigns operated and control for government hospitals with Bolt and Cilliers' data. Where missions took part in state campaigns, I code that as a separate exposure. In Zambia, the fieldwork asks how people remember each. ### F. Mediation

**Q27 ★ Formal mediation needs strong assumptions. Why should we believe your decomposition?**
It needs the assumption that, given history and controls, nothing else jointly drives trust and outcomes. That's strong, and I won't hide it. Three things follow. My primary result is the total effect. I estimate the effect that remains when trust is held fixed using sequential g-estimation (Acharya, Blackwell and Sen), which handles confounders that are themselves affected by history. And I report a sensitivity analysis showing how much hidden confounding would erase the trust share. I'll present it as a decomposition under stated assumptions, checked against the fieldwork.

**Q28 ★ Trust, behaviour and burden come from different surveys. How can you do mediation across them?**
I won't pretend to do individual-level mediation across surveys. Within the DHS, trust (blood-test refusal) and behaviour are in the same sample, so that link is individual-level. Afrobarometer trust is linked at district level, and burden at cluster or district level. The decomposition is done at a common geography, and I'm explicit about the limits of ecological inference.
